Technology can drain the meaning out of work by quietly moving where understanding lives. It used to live in the mechanical: in the hands, in the senses, in the feel of something pushing back. Now it lives in computerized control. Our own interpretive muscles weaken while the software does the interpreting. Nobody decides this. Each handoff is a small, sensible convenience. Added up, you become the person who presses the button.

What’s special about working with the thing itself is that it argues back. Wood splits along the grain you missed. A sentence lands incorrectly and fails to convey your meaning. A patient’s story doesn’t exactly match the chart. That kind of contact with reality demands dialogue and through it you play a game with reality. Run it through black boxes of statistics and software and the dialogue stops. You get an answer instead of an encounter. The output improves, and the person producing it matters less: individual skill, and the identity built around it, is traded for better average performance across everyone using the software. It’s a good trade for the system. It’s a strange one for the individual, who ends up with a résumé of results and a shrinking inventory of individual abilities.

Take music. A century ago, if you wanted music in the house, someone in the house had to make it. The parlor piano was furniture and credential at once, proof of some intimacy with the thing. Then came the radio and the record player, and the piano became a place to put photographs. Playing was replaced by a needle scrolling over the skill of another. Music got better and more plentiful; musicianship got rarer. We gained Wagner and lost the uncle who could could somehow get through three of his songs.

The pattern generalizes. We’ve inherited conceptual and physical tools we can no longer make. Leonard Read pointed out that no single person knows how to make a pencil. Thomas Thwaites spent months building a toaster from scratch, ore and all, and got something that worked briefly before it melted. We assemble results we trust but can’t personally reproduce. Civilization is, in a sense, a giant agreement not to ask how anything works. Mostly that’s fine; it’s why we aren’t all subsistence farmers. The real question is which kinds of ignorance cost nothing and which ones cost something we wanted to keep.

Large language models blur the line between information and skill more than any technology I've used before it. Information could be looked up and a skill had to be grown by ingesting that information. An encyclopedia could tell you what a sonnet is but couldn’t write one. A model can, and it can insidiously make you feel like you wrote it while also sucking all the creativity out of it. It generates on the fly from pretrained weights, the crystallized knowledge of the past brought back to life to do something that looks a lot like fluid thinking. And unlike earlier black boxes, this one talks back. It will clarify, object and revise, which is everything I said gave real contact its meaning. But the conversation is with a summary of everyone, not with the thing itself. It argues back, but on behalf of the average.

Medicine is where this stops being an aesthetic complaint. AI may reduce the accuracy of any one diagnostician. In one 2025 study, endoscopists who had grown used to AI assistance found fewer precancerous polyps once it was switched off. The muscle weakened exactly as predicted. And yet the digital centaur, clinician and model reading together, still leads to better diagnoses overall. (The term comes from Kasparov’s human-plus-computer chess after Deep Blue.) For the patient, that’s the number that matters. I wouldn’t trade a missed tumor for a radiologist’s sense of self.

But the centaur has a structural weakness. It only beats the machine alone while the human half can still see something the machine can’t. Deskill the human enough and you don’t have a centaur; you have a horse with a passenger. So the question isn’t whether to use the tools. I will, gladly. It’s which muscles I keep exercising on purpose, knowing the tool would be faster. The parlor piano didn’t disappear because recordings were bad. It disappeared because nobody had a reason to practice, and the practicing was the point.
