Crystallized by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck: changes in an animal's morphology from use (or disuse) accumulate and lead to differences between species.

Wrong, but significant — it questioned the permanence of species, whether they could change at all.

The textbook example is the giraffe: stretch toward high leaves, the neck lengthens a little, and the offspring inherit the stretch. Darwin's alternative needs no effort from the giraffe — variation already exists, and the longer-necked ones simply leave more descendants ([Darwin's Idea](/notes/darwin-s-idea), [Galapagos Finch](/notes/galapagos-finch)).

Heredity turned out messier than either picture: "like produces like," and "like does not always produce like." *— Knopik et al., Behavioral Genetics* Lamarck lost the argument on inheritance, but the idea keeps reappearing in softer forms — epigenetic marks, and the intuition behind most self-improvement that effort gets written into what you become. Within one lifetime that's roughly true; across a lineage it mostly isn't. <span class="link-private" title="Not public yet">Nature Via Nurture</span> covers the nuance.
