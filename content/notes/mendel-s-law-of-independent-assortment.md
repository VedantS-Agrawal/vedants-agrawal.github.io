The law: inheriting one allele doesn't affect inheriting another.

In reality, false. Alleles can be *linked* — having one makes you more likely to have the other — because of where they sit on the chromosome. Genes close together tend to stay together through recombination. The distance is measured in centimorgans.

That exception is useful: linkage helps locate genes. Once a gene's location is mapped, genetic testing becomes possible, and its protein product can be studied toward a therapeutic.

Two single-gene examples of Mendel's first law:
- **Huntington disease** — dominant; 50% of an affected parent's children inherit it. Starts in middle adulthood with personality changes, forgetfulness, involuntary movements; ends in loss of motor control and intellect.
- **Phenylketonuria** — recessive; the enzyme that processes phenylalanine doesn't work, causing cognitive deficits. Found when blood tests showed high phenylalanine in people with intellectual disability.
