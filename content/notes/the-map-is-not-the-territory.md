A map of reality isn't reality — maps are imperfect.

We need abstractions: descriptions, theories, models. But every map has three problems:
- It can be *incorrect* without our realizing it.
- It's a *reduction* of the real thing, so it loses fidelity.
- It needs *interpretation*, which can introduce major errors.

The phrase is Alfred Korzybski's, and its usefulness lies in how often it's forgotten. A diagnosis is a map of a patient, a model a map of data, a self-image a map of a person, and each one quietly becomes the thing it describes if nobody checks it against the ground.

The fix isn't to stop making maps — reasoning without them is impossible — but to remember which is which and to update when the ground disagrees. [The Voice Inside Your Head](/notes/the-voice-inside-your-head) is a map-maker that never stops drawing; [Emic and etic](/notes/emic-and-etic) is about whose map you're using; [Principal Component Analysis](/notes/principal-component-analysis) is a deliberately lossy one, chosen for what it leaves out.
