Instruction that helps learners build long-term schemas works best for novices, who have no such structures to lean on. For experts, less guidance often beats well-guided instruction.

Same reason the [Worked Example Effect](/notes/worked-example-effect) fades with prior knowledge: once a problem-solving schema exists, giving it again is redundant.

The awkward implication is that there's no single best way to teach something; the best way depends on who's in the room. Guidance that holds a novice's hand is, for an expert, just extra material to read and reconcile with what they already know — extraneous load by another name, the same cost as the [Split Attention Effect](/notes/split-attention-effect).

The fix is fading: start with full worked solutions, move to partly finished ones ([Completion-Problem Effect](/notes/completion-problem-effect)), then let go entirely. It's also a decent case for skipping the introductory chapters of anything already familiar, and for suspicion of any teaching that never changes as the learner does ([Cognitive Load](/notes/cognitive-load)).
