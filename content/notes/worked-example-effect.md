Novices do better on transfer tasks — using skills in unfamiliar situations — when taught with worked examples than with problem-solving.

One reason: less extraneous load. Solving a problem from scratch means figuring out *how* to solve it before you can learn the schema worth storing.

I find this interesting next to [The Shallows](/library/the-shallows-what-the-internet-is-doing-to-our-brains), where people who learned a game with minimal instruction learned it more robustly than people guided by software. Could the long-term robustness of learning differ between the two groups here too?

And per the Scott H. Young podcast I referenced in [Cognitive Load](/notes/cognitive-load), people with more prior knowledge benefit less — the material is redundant. With a problem-solving schema already in long-term memory, they can segment the problem efficiently and spend working memory on solving it. See [Expertise Reversal Effect](/notes/expertise-reversal-effect).
