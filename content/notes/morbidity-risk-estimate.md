The chance of being affected by a disorder over an entire lifetime, age-corrected — because the risk of getting a disease often depends on age. Schizophrenia, for example, doesn't show until late adolescence or later.

Schizophrenia lifetime risk by relation to someone affected: second-degree relative 4%, first-degree 9%, fraternal twin 17%, identical twin 48%.

The schizophrenia numbers are the textbook case for genetic influence: risk climbs with relatedness. Galton would have liked them — "nature prevails enormously over nurture" *— Francis Galton, quoted in Behavioral Genetics* — but they're also the textbook case for the limits. Identical twins share essentially all their genes, and the concordance still stops near half.

Whatever makes up the other half isn't in the sequence, which is [Nature vs Nurture](/notes/nature-vs-nurture) in two numbers; <span class="link-private" title="Not public yet">Nature Via Nurture</span> explains why the two terms resist separation. The age correction matters for the same reason survival statistics do: without it, a nineteen-year-old who hasn't yet developed the disorder counts as unaffected, and the risk gets quietly understated. Source: <span class="link-private" title="Not public yet">Behavioral Genetics</span>.
