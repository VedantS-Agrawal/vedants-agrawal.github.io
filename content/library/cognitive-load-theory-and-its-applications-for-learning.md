Notes in [Cognitive Load](/notes/cognitive-load) and [Worked Example Effect](/notes/worked-example-effect).

The idea at the center is simple enough to be easy to ignore: working memory is tiny, and the way material is presented decides how much of it is left over for actual learning. Everything practical follows — the [Split Attention Effect](/notes/split-attention-effect) when text and diagram live apart, the [Worked Example Effect](/notes/worked-example-effect) when novices study solutions before attempting problems, and the [Expertise Reversal Effect](/notes/expertise-reversal-effect) when the same help becomes clutter for someone who already knows the material.

The upshot is unglamorous. Cut what's extraneous, sequence from worked examples to independent problems, and stop mistaking struggle for depth when most of the struggle is with the layout. It's also a quiet argument against learning from a browser full of tabs; see [The Shallows](/library/the-shallows-what-the-internet-is-doing-to-our-brains).
