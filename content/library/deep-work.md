- **Great Restructuring** — disruptive technology lowers the value of an individual's labor; employers will increasingly hire machines over people. Further reading: Tyler Cowen's *Average Is Over*.
- **Attention residue** — after switching tasks, working memory and attention stay partly on the last one, and performance on the new one drops.
- **Distracted high performers** — few decision-making roles don't need the quiet of deep work. CEOs are paid for their judgment on solutions, not for producing them.

Newport's core claim is economic before it's personal: "the ability to perform deep work is becoming increasingly rare at exactly the same time it is becoming increasingly valuable in our economy." *— Cal Newport, Deep Work* The Great Restructuring is the demand side of that sentence. Attention residue is the supply-side problem: every quick check of email leaves part of the mind behind, so a day of short switches is a day of half-attention.

The practical version lives in [Give the morning's best hours to deep work](/notes/give-the-morning-s-best-hours-to-deep-work); the temperamental version in [Hurry leaves no room for depth](/notes/hurry-leaves-no-room-for-depth). [The Shallows](/library/the-shallows-what-the-internet-is-doing-to-our-brains) makes the same case from the neuroscience side, and [Decision quality falls as decision count rises](/notes/decision-quality-falls-as-decision-count-rises) explains why Newport leans on rituals — fixed times, fixed places — to do so much of the work: they remove the decision to start.
