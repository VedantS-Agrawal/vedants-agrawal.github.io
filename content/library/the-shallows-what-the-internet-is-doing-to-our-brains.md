Carr, after McLuhan: outside of the content a medium carries, the medium itself changes our patterns of perception — how we think as individuals and as a society.

- The internet lets us offload memory and think more interrelationally. That offloading probably comes at a cost: less ability to read and understand long-form, and more trouble remembering what we've handed off to silicon.
- Plasticity cuts both ways — [Monkey Brain Mapping Experiment](/notes/monkey-brain-mapping-experiment). "We can become, neurologically, what we think."
- [Time-Keeping](/notes/time-keeping): for most of history, time was a continuous, cyclical flow. Things just took as long as they took; there wasn't a way to measure whether something was done "better."
- [Progression of Language](/notes/progression-of-language): the phonetic alphabet made an economy of characters. Deep reading needed a shift from bottom-up to top-down attention.
- The internet seizes attention only to scatter it. My read: what we lose is the ability to sit with one idea for a long time — shorter attention spans, shallower dives, bite-sized messages as the norm of communication and of understanding.
- Linear readers comprehend more, remember more, learn more than readers of hyperlinked text. [Effects of Hyperlinks on Learning](/notes/effects-of-hyperlinks-on-learning), [Cognitive Load](/notes/cognitive-load).
- When journals moved online, scholars cited fewer and more recent articles — the opposite of what you'd expect.
- [Technological Determinists vs Instrumentalists](/notes/technological-determinists-vs-instrumentalists).
